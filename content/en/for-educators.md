# Shape How the Next Generation Learns with AI

**You're on the front line of AI in education.** Every day, you make decisions about how technology shapes young minds. This guide helps you use AI responsibly, protecting students while making room for what genuinely helps them learn.

AI in education isn't just a technical question. It's a pedagogical one, an ethical one, and increasingly, a legal one. The good news: the most important expertise here is yours. You know your students. You don't need to be an engineer to decide whether a tool serves them.

---

## Your Concerns Are Valid

### "How do I use AI responsibly in teaching?"
You want to use AI's potential without compromising learning outcomes or student wellbeing.

### "What about ChatGPT and academic integrity?"
Generative AI is transforming how students work. You need practical approaches that focus on learning, not just detection.

### "What tools are safe to use with students?"
With vendors making big promises, you need to know what questions to ask and what red flags to watch for.

### "What are my legal obligations?"
Some EU AI Act rules already apply to schools today. Stricter rules for high-risk education AI start on 2 December 2027.

---

## What the Law Says Today (November 2026)

- **Emotion recognition is banned in education (since 2 February 2025).** AI that infers students' (or staff's) emotions in schools and other educational settings is prohibited, except for medical or safety reasons. If a proctoring or "engagement" tool claims to detect boredom, stress or attention from faces or voices, ask hard questions.
- **AI literacy (rewritten in July 2026).** Every organisation that uses AI, of any size, must take measures to support the AI literacy of its staff. For a school, that means training and support so teachers understand the tools they use. It does not require every individual to reach a fixed level.
- **Transparency (since 2 August 2026).** Chatbots must tell users they are AI, and deepfakes must be labelled. This matters both for tools you use and for media literacy lessons. [More on AI transparency →](/en/rights/ai-transparency)
- **GDPR applies now.** Student data in AI tools is personal data. Students and parents have rights, including a meaningful explanation where a decision with significant effects is made solely by an automated system.

## What's Coming

| Date | What changes for schools |
|------|--------------------------|
| **2 December 2026** | Ban on AI that generates realistic intimate or sexual images of identifiable people without consent, including child sexual abuse material ("nudifier" apps). Highly relevant to pupils. |
| **2 December 2027** | AI used to decide admission or access to education, evaluate learning outcomes or monitor students during tests becomes **high-risk**, with requirements for risk management, human oversight, documentation and transparency. |

These high-risk rules were originally due in August 2026. The EU's "Digital Omnibus" law, in force since July 2026, delayed them so that technical standards and guidance can be ready. That is extra time to prepare, not a reason to wait: the ban on emotion recognition, AI literacy and GDPR already apply.

### 📖 AI Literacy: Frameworks and Teaching Approaches

Teaching students to think critically about AI is increasingly seen as a core competency. Some practical approaches:

**Understand before you use:** Help students understand what AI tools are: statistical systems trained on data, not oracles. Demystifying AI reduces both uncritical adoption and unfounded fear.

**Process-centred assessment:** Focus on the learning process, not just outputs. Ask students to document their reasoning, revisions and sources.

**Discuss the model:** For chatbot tools, discuss how language models work, what "hallucination" means and why outputs must be verified.

**Spot the label:** Since August 2026, deepfakes must be labelled in the EU. Ask students to find examples, and discuss what labels can and cannot tell us.

**Use the EU AI Act as a teaching case:** The regulation raises great classroom questions about how we define risk, who is protected and who should be accountable. The 2026 delay of the high-risk rules is a good debate topic in itself.

### ⚖️ Ethical Considerations When Using AI in Teaching

**Transparency with students:** Are students aware when AI shapes their learning or assessments? It's ethically important and, for some systems, a legal requirement.

**Equity and access:** Not all students have equal access to AI tools at home. Policies that require AI-assisted work may disadvantage some.

**Data collection:** Many tools collect detailed data on student behaviour. Ask what is collected, who owns it and how long it is kept.

**Autonomy and critical thinking:** Heavy AI use can reduce the productive struggle that builds real understanding. Balance matters.

**Student wellbeing:** Emotion recognition in education is banned. Other monitoring tools, like attention tracking, still deserve careful scrutiny.

**The human factor:** AI can save time on routine work so teachers have more time for students. Keep a human in charge of anything that affects a student's grades, progress or wellbeing.

## Why This Matters for You

From 2 December 2027, AI that determines access to education or evaluates students will be **high-risk** under the EU AI Act, and must meet strict requirements for fairness, transparency and human oversight. Some protections, like the ban on emotion recognition, already apply.

As an educator, you're in a unique position. You can:

- **Protect students** by asking the right questions before tools are adopted
- **Model responsible AI use** by being open about how you use these tools
- **Advocate for better practices** within your institution
- **Prepare students** for a world where AI literacy is essential

AI can serve learning, but only if people like you stay in charge of how it is used.

---

## Your Next Steps

1. **Audit your current AI use.** List every AI tool you use with students. For each: do students and parents know? What data is collected? Does it infer emotions? (If so, stop and raise it.)
2. **Ask for AI literacy support.** Ask your school leadership what measures are in place to build staff AI literacy, as the AI Act now expects.
3. **Prepare for December 2027.** Flag tools used for admissions, grading or test monitoring. They will be high-risk.
4. **Talk about deepfakes.** Before the ban on 2 December 2026, agree with colleagues how the school will respond if a pupil is targeted.
5. **Start one conversation.** Ask your students what they think about AI. Their perspective may surprise you. Then explore the [Educator Guidance](/en/for-organizations/educators).
