# Implement AI That Serves All Citizens

**When government uses AI, the stakes are higher.** Citizens can't choose a different provider. Public services affect fundamental rights. And public trust, once broken, is hard to rebuild.

Done well, AI can make public services faster, more consistent and more accessible, freeing staff to spend time on people rather than paperwork. The human factor is what makes the difference: the caseworker who reviews the decision, the manager who asks whether a tool is fair, the citizen who is allowed to object. You don't need to be an engineer to lead that. You need to ask the right questions.

This guide helps public officials and policymakers deploy AI responsibly under the EU AI Act as it stands in November 2026.

---

## Your Responsibilities

### Comply with the AI Act as Deployers
Public sector organisations are mostly "deployers" under the EU AI Act: organisations that use AI under their own authority. Some duties already apply; the strictest, for high-risk AI, start on 2 December 2027.

### Protect Citizen Rights
Citizens have the right to a meaningful explanation and human review of significant automated decisions under the GDPR, and from 2 December 2027 to be told when high-risk AI is used in decisions about them.

### Make Informed Policy Decisions
Understanding AI's capabilities and limitations is essential for sound policy.

### Build Public Trust
Transparent, accountable AI use strengthens democratic legitimacy. Opaque systems erode it.

## What Applies to Public Bodies Today (November 2026)

- **Prohibited practices (since 2 February 2025).** Stop any banned use immediately.
- **AI literacy (rewritten July 2026).** Every organisation using AI must take measures to support the AI literacy of its staff. This applies to all public bodies using AI, whatever the risk level. It does not require guaranteeing a specific level for each person.
- **Transparency (since 2 August 2026).** Government chatbots must tell people they are AI. AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest (unless the text has been reviewed by a person and your organisation takes editorial responsibility for it), and deepfakes, must be labelled. If you use emotion recognition or biometric categorisation where lawful, people must be informed.
- **Enforcement (since 2 August 2026).** National market surveillance authorities and the EU AI Office now supervise and enforce.
- **GDPR.** Decisions about citizens made solely by automated means with legal or similarly significant effects fall under GDPR Art. 22. The EU Court of Justice confirmed in February 2025 (C-203/22) that people have a right to a meaningful explanation of how such a decision was reached. The AI Act's own right to an explanation and duty to inform people about high-risk AI use apply from 2 December 2027.

## Upcoming Deadlines

| Date | What's required |
|------|-----------------|
| **2 December 2026** | New ban on AI generating non-consensual intimate images or child sexual abuse material. Platform Work Directive transposition deadline (for ministries responsible). |
| **2 August 2027** | Each Member State must have at least one AI regulatory sandbox operational |
| **2 December 2027** | High-risk rules apply to Annex III systems (Annex III is the list of high-risk areas in the law), including access to public services and benefits, law enforcement, migration and border control, justice and democratic processes, education and employment |
| **2 August 2028** | High-risk rules for AI embedded in regulated products |
| **2 August 2030** | High-risk AI systems **already used by public authorities** must comply |

These dates reflect the **Digital Omnibus on AI** (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026). It moved the high-risk deadlines (previously August 2026 and August 2027) to give time for harmonised standards and guidance. Only the first harmonised standard (EN 18286, quality management) has been cited so far, and the Commission's guidelines on classifying high-risk AI are still in draft. Use the extra time to prepare properly, not to pause. [Full timeline →](/en/timeline)

#### Prohibited Uses
Certain AI applications are banned entirely, and public bodies must be especially vigilant.

*Key prohibitions for the public sector:*
- Social scoring
- Real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement (with narrow exceptions)
- Predicting criminal behaviour based solely on profiling
- Emotion recognition in workplaces and education (except for medical or safety reasons)
- From 2 December 2026: AI systems that generate realistic intimate images of identifiable people without consent, or child sexual abuse material, without effective safeguards against it

[Public Sector Guide (Prohibited Uses) →](/en/for-organizations/public-sector)

#### High-Risk Obligations
Many public sector AI uses will be high-risk from 2 December 2027. Requirements include risk management, documentation, human oversight and registration in the EU database. Note that Annex III systems that a provider judges not to be high-risk must still be registered, with less information.

## Policy Tools for Implementation

### Fundamental Rights Impact Assessments (FRIA)
Public bodies (and private bodies providing public services) deploying high-risk AI listed in Annex III, except critical infrastructure, must carry out a **Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment** before first use and notify the results to the market surveillance authority. This applies from 2 December 2027 for new systems; existing public sector systems must comply by 2 August 2030. The AI Office is to provide a template questionnaire. Starting early is good practice:
- Identify which fundamental rights the system could affect
- Assess the likelihood and severity of harm
- Document mitigation measures and human oversight
- Consult affected communities where appropriate

### Regulatory Sandboxes
**AI regulatory sandboxes** are supervised environments where AI can be tested before full deployment. Each Member State must have at least one operational by **2 August 2027** (a deadline moved from 2026). They allow:
- Testing in real conditions with regulatory oversight
- Spotting compliance issues early
- Building evidence for risk assessments

Contact your national AI authority about sandbox plans in your country.

### Technical Standards
- **CEN/CENELEC** is developing harmonised standards for the AI Act. The first, **EN 18286** (quality management systems), was cited in the Official Journal in October 2026. Standards on risk management, data, logging, accuracy and cybersecurity are still in progress.
- **ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42** develops international AI standards.
- **ENISA** provides guidance on AI cybersecurity.

## National Authorities and Coordination

### The national authority role
Member States had to designate national authorities by 2 August 2025, and those authorities have been able to enforce since 2 August 2026. Many were late: by mid-June 2026 only 9 of 27 had designated both market surveillance and notifying authorities. If you work in central government, check where your country stands.

### Example: Denmark
Denmark's AI Act implementing law (Lov nr. 467 of 14 May 2025, in force 2 August 2025) gives a clear model:
- **Digitaliseringsstyrelsen** (Agency for Digital Government) is the single point of contact, notifying authority and a market surveillance authority
- **Datatilsynet** (Data Protection Agency) supervises certain prohibited practices
- **Domstolsstyrelsen** (Court Administration) covers the courts' administrative AI use

### Sharing the governance burden

**Within your organisation:**
- Designate an AI coordination function (a working group is fine)
- Involve legal, IT, service delivery, communications and frontline staff
- Create an escalation path for novel or high-risk deployments

**Across government:**
- Share impact assessments and risk registers with central digital or AI authorities
- Coordinate procurement requirements for shared standards
- Join cross-government AI working groups where available

**With external bodies:**
- Engage your national data protection authority and AI authority on compliance questions
- Report significant AI-related incidents to the relevant authorities

## Why This Matters

**Public sector AI affects people who have no choice.** Citizens can't take their business elsewhere if government AI fails them. That creates a special obligation for fairness and accountability, rooted in power asymmetry, equal treatment, fundamental rights and democratic accountability.

Responsible AI isn't just about compliance. It's about keeping the trust that democratic governance requires, and showing that AI can serve citizens rather than just cut costs.

---

## Your Next Steps

1. **Inventory your AI.** List every AI system across your organisation, including chatbots and generative AI tools. You can't manage what you don't know about.
2. **Check for prohibited uses now.** Stop anything banned, and before 2 December 2026 check that any image-generating AI you use has effective safeguards against producing non-consensual intimate images.
3. **Confirm transparency today.** Make sure chatbots disclose they are AI and AI-generated public information is labelled.
4. **Put AI literacy measures in place.** Training and support for staff who use or oversee AI.
5. **Plan for 2027 and 2030.** Classify systems, start FRIAs and update procurement terms. Then use the [Public Sector Guide](/en/for-organizations/public-sector) and [Compliance Checklist](/en/for-organizations/compliance-checklist).
