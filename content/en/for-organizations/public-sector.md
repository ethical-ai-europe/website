# AI in the Public Sector

**When government uses AI, the stakes are higher.** Citizens can't choose a different provider. Public services affect fundamental rights. And public trust, once broken, is hard to rebuild.

This guide helps government officials, public administrators and policymakers use AI responsibly. The public sector faces heightened obligations under the EU AI Act, and rightly so: when algorithms shape decisions about benefits, services or enforcement, they must be fair, transparent and accountable.

> **Update, November 2026:** The Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744), in force since 27 July 2026, moved high-risk rules for uses such as benefits, migration and law enforcement to **2 December 2027**. High-risk systems that public authorities were **already using** must comply by **2 August 2030** at the latest. **The deadline moved, the direction didn't.** Citizens' expectations of fair, explainable public decisions apply today, and so does the GDPR.

*This guide is not legal advice. Involve your organisation's legal service and data protection officer in specific decisions.*

---

## What Applies to Public Bodies Right Now

- **Prohibited practices** (since February 2025): see the hard lines below.
- **AI literacy** (rule rewritten July 2026): every organisation using AI must *take measures to support* its staff's AI literacy. There's no guaranteed level for each person, but caseworkers and managers using AI need real training.
- **Transparency** (since 2 August 2026): chatbots and virtual assistants on public websites must make clear they are AI; deepfakes and AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest must be labelled; people exposed to emotion recognition or biometric categorisation must be informed.
- **New ban from 2 December 2026** on AI that generates intimate images of real people without consent.
- **Enforcement:** national authorities have been able to enforce since 2 August 2026 (the EU AI Office supervises general-purpose AI providers and certain large platforms). Each Member State decides whether, and how far, fines can be imposed on public authorities (Article 99(8)).

## Why Public Sector AI Faces Higher Standards

Public sector AI carries unique responsibilities:

- **No alternative provider:** Citizens can't take their business elsewhere if government AI fails them
- **Power asymmetry:** Government has coercive power that private entities lack
- **Equal treatment obligations:** Government must treat all citizens fairly
- **Fundamental rights:** Government decisions often affect protected rights
- **Democratic accountability:** Citizens have the right to understand how they're governed
- **Public trust:** Government credibility depends on fair, transparent processes

The EU AI Act recognises this. Many public sector AI applications are classified as **high-risk** or even **prohibited**.

## Prohibited Uses: Hard Lines

Certain AI applications are banned entirely. Public sector organisations must be especially vigilant:

### ❌ Social Scoring
AI systems that evaluate people based on social behaviour or personality characteristics, leading to unjustified or disproportionate treatment, for example in access to public services.

**This includes:** Systems that aggregate data about citizen behaviour to create scores affecting service access, even if not called "social scoring."

### ❌ Real-Time Remote Biometric Identification for Law Enforcement
Live facial recognition or other real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement purposes is prohibited, with very narrow exceptions:
- Targeted search for specific crime victims (including missing children)
- Prevention of specific, imminent threats such as terrorist attacks
- Locating suspects of serious crimes

**Even exceptions require:** Judicial or independent administrative authorisation, strict necessity and proportionality. See also [Facial recognition: your rights](/en/rights/facial-recognition).

### ❌ Predictive Policing Based Solely on Profiling
AI systems that predict whether a person will commit a crime based solely on profiling or personality traits, without objective, verifiable facts linked to criminal activity.

### ❌ Untargeted Scraping of Facial Images
Building or expanding facial recognition databases by untargeted scraping of images from the internet or CCTV.

### ❌ Manipulation and Exploiting Vulnerabilities
AI that uses manipulative or deceptive techniques, or exploits people's vulnerabilities due to age, disability or social or economic situation, to distort their behaviour in ways that cause or are likely to cause significant harm.

### ❌ Emotion Recognition at Work and in Education
Emotion recognition in workplaces and educational institutions is prohibited, except for medical or safety reasons. This covers public employers and public schools, and the Commission reads "workplace" broadly, including job candidates.

### ❌ Biometric Categorisation Using Sensitive Attributes
AI systems that categorise individuals based on biometric data to infer race, political opinions, religious beliefs, trade union membership, sex life or sexual orientation.

### ❌ Non-Consensual Intimate Deepfakes (from 2 December 2026)
AI systems that generate or manipulate realistic intimate or sexually explicit images of identifiable people without their consent, including child sexual abuse material. See [Intimate deepfakes](/en/rights/intimate-deepfakes).

## Framework for Responsible Public Sector AI

When considering any AI deployment, work through this framework:

### 1. Necessity
**Question:** Is AI actually needed to achieve the public interest objective?

- What problem are you trying to solve?
- Can it be solved without AI?
- What's the evidence that AI will perform better than alternatives?
- Have you considered non-technical solutions?

**Guidance:** AI should solve real problems, not create solutions in search of problems. Start with the policy objective, not the technology.

### 2. Proportionality
**Question:** Is AI the least invasive means to achieve the objective?

- Does the AI use go beyond what's necessary?
- Could less data-intensive or less impactful approaches work?
- Is the scope appropriately limited?
- Have you considered less intrusive alternatives?

**Guidance:** More powerful AI isn't always better. Use the minimum capability needed.

### 3. Transparency
**Question:** Can citizens understand how the system works and how it affects them?

- Can you explain the system in plain language?
- Will affected individuals be told when AI is used in their case?
- Is documentation publicly accessible?
- Can citizens understand why they received a particular outcome?

**Guidance:** Citizens have the right to understand how they're governed. Under the GDPR, the EU Court of Justice has confirmed that people are entitled to a meaningful explanation of the procedure and principles behind an automated decision (solely automated, with legal or similarly significant effects) (Dun & Bradstreet Austria, 2025). If you can't explain it, reconsider deploying it.

### 4. Accountability
**Question:** Who is responsible when something goes wrong?

- Is there a clear chain of responsibility?
- Who makes the final decision (a human, not AI)?
- What happens when errors occur?
- How are systemic problems identified and corrected?

**Guidance:** Accountability can't be delegated to an algorithm. A human must be responsible.

### 5. Redress
**Question:** How can citizens challenge AI-influenced decisions?

- Can individuals request human review?
- Is there an appeals process?
- Do citizens know their rights?
- Are redress mechanisms accessible to all, not just the technically sophisticated?

**Guidance:** The right to challenge government decisions is fundamental. AI can't be a black box that citizens can't contest. See [Your right to an explanation](/en/rights/right-to-explanation).

## Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (FRIA)

Before using a high-risk AI system, public bodies (and certain other deployers) must assess its impact on fundamental rights. In plain terms, a FRIA asks: who could be affected, how could they be harmed, what human oversight is in place, and what will you do if things go wrong?

- The AI Office is to provide a **template questionnaire** to make this easier. Use it when it is available.
- Do the FRIA early, ideally during procurement, so its findings can shape the contract and the system design.
- Link it to your GDPR data protection impact assessment rather than doing the work twice.

This applies from 2 December 2027 (Annex III systems other than critical infrastructure). You must notify the results to the market surveillance authority (Article 27). Besides public bodies, it also covers private organisations providing public services and any deployer using AI for credit scoring of individuals or for life and health insurance risk and pricing.

## Procurement Guidance

Systems you buy today will still be running when the high-risk rules apply in December 2027. Write the requirements into contracts now.

### Compliance Documentation
- EU AI Act risk classification and its rationale
- A roadmap to conformity assessment (a check, by the provider or an independent body, that the system meets the rules) and CE marking (the label showing it does) for high-risk systems (required for new high-risk systems from 2 December 2027)
- Technical documentation and instructions for use meeting AI Act requirements
- Information on quality management. The first harmonised standard (an official European technical standard; following it is a recognised way to show you meet the rules), EN 18286 on quality management systems, is available; others are still in development

### Fairness and Bias
- Bias testing results across relevant demographic groups
- Information about training data diversity
- Ongoing monitoring commitments
- Performance data broken down by affected groups
- Where sensitive data is needed to detect and correct bias, confirmation that it's handled under the new Article 4a safeguards (strictly necessary, protected, deleted afterwards)

### Transparency and Explainability
- Plain-language system description
- Explanation of decision factors
- User-facing explanations suitable for citizens
- Documentation in accessible formats
- AI disclosure built into any chatbot or virtual assistant

### Data Protection
- GDPR compliance documentation
- Data processing agreements
- Security certifications
- Breach notification procedures

### Accountability and Support
- Clear liability terms (note the new Product Liability Directive covering software, including AI, applies from 9 December 2026)
- Error handling procedures
- Performance guarantees
- Ongoing support commitments
- Exit strategy: what happens if you switch vendors?

### Contract Terms to Include
- Right to audit AI systems
- Requirements for ongoing bias monitoring
- Transparency about significant system changes
- Data portability and deletion requirements
- Performance benchmarks with consequences for failure
- Vendor cooperation with your FRIA and with registration

## EU Database Registration

The EU AI Act requires high-risk AI systems to be registered in an EU database, and public authorities using high-risk systems have their own registration duties. Systems in the high-risk areas that a provider has judged *not* to be high-risk (under Article 6(3)) must still be registered, with less information.

### What Must Be Registered
- Description of the AI system
- Intended purpose
- Status of the system
- Contact information
- Key information about risk management and use
- For public-authority deployers: a summary of the fundamental rights impact assessment and of the data protection impact assessment

### When to Register
- Before putting a high-risk system into service, once the high-risk rules apply (2 December 2027 for most public uses)
- Updates required for significant changes

### Public Access
Much of the register will be publicly accessible, so citizens can see which high-risk AI systems public authorities use. Entries for law enforcement, migration and border systems go in a non-public section.

**This is an opportunity, not just an obligation.** Proactive transparency about AI use builds public trust. You don't have to wait for 2027 to publish your own list of AI systems.

## Human Oversight Implementation

For high-risk public sector AI, human oversight will be mandatory. It is also simply good administration today. Here's how to implement it effectively:

### Meaningful Oversight
- Humans must be able to understand AI outputs
- Decision-makers must be trained on system limitations
- Overriding AI should be genuinely possible, not just theoretical
- Time and resources must be allocated for human review

### Avoid "Automation Bias"
People tend to defer to AI recommendations (this is called automation bias). Counter it by:
- Training staff to critically evaluate AI outputs
- Requiring independent judgment before viewing AI recommendations
- Tracking override rates and investigating if they are very low
- Rewarding thoughtful review, not rubber-stamping

### Clear Procedures
Document:
- Who reviews AI outputs before decisions are made?
- Under what circumstances should AI be overridden?
- How are overrides recorded and reviewed?
- How is the AI updated based on override patterns?

### Capacity
Ensure:
- Enough staff to review AI-influenced decisions
- Realistic workloads that allow genuine review
- Training on AI system capabilities and limitations
- Support for staff who identify AI errors

## Phased Plan for Public Bodies

**Now**
- Build an inventory of AI systems in use, including AI features in existing case management and office software
- Confirm no prohibited practices are in use
- Put AI literacy measures in place for staff, with deeper training for caseworkers and managers using AI
- Check transparency: chatbots disclose they are AI; public-interest AI-generated text and deepfakes are labelled
- Make sure citizens can get a human review of any AI-assisted decision

**By 2 December 2026**
- Confirm no tool you use or fund can generate intimate images of real people without consent
- Review procurement and liability terms in light of the Product Liability Directive (9 December 2026)

**Before 2 December 2027**
- Classify every system against the high-risk areas (benefits, essential services, law enforcement, migration, justice and more), and re-check when the Commission's final high-risk guidelines are adopted
- Complete FRIAs for new high-risk systems, using the AI Office template
- Prepare EU database registration
- Require conformity documentation and instructions for use from vendors

**By 2 August 2028**
- High-risk requirements apply to AI built into regulated products (for example medical devices in public hospitals)

**By 2 August 2030 at the latest**
- High-risk systems already in use by public authorities must comply. Plan the upgrade or replacement now; don't leave it to the final year.

See the full [EU AI Act timeline](/en/timeline).

## Case Studies

*These are illustrative composite examples, not descriptions of specific organisations.*

### 🟢 Good Practice: Transparent Benefits Processing

A social services agency used AI to speed up benefits processing. They:
- Published a plain-language description of the AI system
- Ensured caseworkers reviewed AI recommendations before decisions
- Tracked decision patterns by demographic group to catch bias
- Provided a clear appeals process referencing AI involvement
- Regularly audited system performance and published results

**Result:** Faster processing with maintained fairness and citizen trust.

### 🔴 Problem Case: Opaque Fraud Detection

A tax authority used AI fraud detection without adequate oversight:
- Citizens weren't told AI flagged their returns
- No explanation provided for fraud determinations
- Limited ability to challenge AI classifications
- Bias in fraud predictions went undetected for years
- Disproportionate impact on certain communities

**Result:** Wrongful accusations, broken trust, legal action and programme suspension.

### 🟢 Good Practice: Public Registry for Police AI

A police department using AI for resource allocation:
- Published all AI tools in use on a public website
- Provided plain-language descriptions of each system
- Explained what data is used and how
- Held public meetings to discuss AI use
- Established a civilian oversight board for AI decisions

**Result:** Maintained community trust while using AI effectively.

### 🔴 Problem Case: Educational AI Without Consent

A school district used AI to predict student outcomes:
- Parents weren't informed about AI profiling
- Predictions influenced tracking and access to opportunities
- No way for students or parents to challenge classifications
- Bias in predictions reinforced existing inequalities
- Media exposure caused significant backlash

**Result:** Programme cancelled, trust damaged, legal scrutiny.

## Citizen Communication Templates

### Notification Template
> **AI Assistance in Your [Case/Application/Request]**
>
> [Agency name] uses an AI-assisted system to help process [type of case]. This system helps us [purpose, e.g., "process applications more quickly" or "identify cases that need priority review"].
>
> **What this means for you:**
> - An AI system provided information to assist our staff
> - A trained [title] reviewed your case before any decision was made
> - No decision was made solely by an AI system
>
> **Your rights:**
> - You can request information about how AI was used in your case
> - You can request a full human review of any decision
> - You can appeal any decision through [process]
>
> For questions: [contact information]

> "You're chatting with [name], an AI assistant from [agency]. It can make mistakes. For personal advice or to speak to a person, [contact option]."

## Who Supervises?

Each Member State designates national authorities to supervise the AI Act. Many were late: by mid-June 2026 only 9 of 27 Member States had designated both their market surveillance and notifying authorities. In **Denmark**, the Danish Agency for Digital Government (Digitaliseringsstyrelsen) is the single point of contact and a market surveillance authority, the Danish Data Protection Agency (Datatilsynet) supervises certain bans, and the Danish Court Administration (Domstolsstyrelsen) covers courts' administrative use of AI. The European Commission's [AI Act Service Desk](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/) is a central source of information.

## Key Takeaways

✅ **Public sector AI faces higher standards**, and rightly so

✅ **Know the prohibitions**: some AI uses are completely banned, with a new ban from 2 December 2026

✅ **Know the dates**: high-risk rules from 2 December 2027; existing public systems by 2 August 2030

✅ **Apply the framework**: necessity, proportionality, transparency, accountability, redress

✅ **Assess fundamental rights impact** before using high-risk AI

✅ **Require compliance from vendors**: don't accept vague assurances

✅ **Register high-risk systems**: it's required, and it builds trust

✅ **Implement real human oversight**, not rubber-stamping

✅ **Communicate proactively**: citizens deserve to know
