# Facial Recognition: Where It's Banned

Facial recognition is one of the most regulated AI technologies in Europe. The EU AI Act imposes strict bans and limits on how it can be used, because your face shouldn't be a tracking device. The bans below have applied since **2 February 2025**, and since **2 August 2026** national authorities have had the power to enforce them.

## Understanding Facial Recognition

Not all face-related technology works the same way. Here's what you need to know:

### Types of Facial Recognition

**Real-time identification** - Cameras that identify you as you walk by, comparing your face to a database in the moment. *Banned for law enforcement in publicly accessible spaces, with narrow exceptions. Other uses are not banned by the AI Act but are tightly limited by the GDPR, and will be high-risk from 2 December 2027.*

**Post-facto identification** - Using footage after an event to identify people. *Not banned. Classed as high-risk, with specific authorisation rules for police from 2 December 2027; data protection law already applies.*

**Verification (1-to-1)** - Confirming you are who you claim to be, like unlocking your phone with your face. *Generally allowed with consent.*

**Detection (not recognition)** - Simply detecting that a face is present, without identifying who it belongs to. *Lower risk, but still regulated.*

## What the EU AI Act Prohibits

### ❌ Banned Uses

The following have been **prohibited** across the European Union since 2 February 2025:

**Real-time remote biometric identification by police in publicly accessible spaces**
Law enforcement cannot use AI to identify people in real time through cameras in publicly accessible spaces (streets, stations, shops and other places the public can enter). There are very narrow exceptions for:
- Searching for specific victims of abduction, trafficking or sexual exploitation, and missing persons
- Preventing a specific, imminent threat to life or a terrorist attack
- Locating suspects of serious crimes (listed offences punishable by at least four years in prison)

Even these exceptions are only available if your country's law allows them, and each use needs prior approval by a judge or independent authority (in urgent cases, within 24 hours).

**Scraping facial images**
Building facial recognition databases by untargeted scraping of photos from the internet or CCTV footage is banned. No one may build a face database this way in the EU.

**Emotion recognition in certain contexts**
AI that claims to detect emotions from faces, voices or other biometric data is banned in workplaces and schools, including during job recruitment. The only exceptions are for medical or safety reasons. Official EU guidance from February 2025 reads "workplace" broadly, so job candidates are protected too.

**Biometric categorisation by sensitive attributes**
AI that uses your biometric data (such as your face) to infer race, political opinions, trade union membership, religious or philosophical beliefs, or sex life or sexual orientation is prohibited.

### ⚠️ Restricted Uses

These require strict oversight:

**Post-event law enforcement use** - Not banned. Classed as high-risk, with specific authorisation rules for police from 2 December 2027; data protection law already applies.

**Access control in sensitive facilities** - Allowed with appropriate safeguards

**Identity verification for required services** - Such as banking verification, with consent

**When the stricter "high-risk" rules start:** Many biometric systems that are not banned outright, such as remote identification after the event, are classed as "high-risk" under the AI Act. That means they will need risk checks, human oversight and record-keeping. These rules apply from **2 December 2027** (they were originally due in August 2026, but were postponed by an amending law in 2026). Until then, the bans above and the GDPR's strict rules on biometric data already protect you.

### 🔔 You Must Be Told

Since **2 August 2026**, organisations that use AI for **emotion recognition** or **biometric categorisation** (sorting people into groups based on their face or body) in situations where it is still allowed must **inform the people exposed to it**. If you're being analysed this way and nobody told you, that's a potential breach. [Learn more about AI transparency →](/en/rights/ai-transparency)

## Private Companies: What They Can and Can't Do

### Generally Not Allowed

- Tracking customers through stores using facial recognition
- Building profiles of visitors without explicit consent
- Using emotion detection on employees or job candidates (banned under the AI Act since February 2025, except for medical or safety reasons)
- Using emotion detection on customers without telling them (you must be informed; GDPR limits also apply)
- Sharing biometric data with third parties

### May Be Allowed with Proper Consent

- Voluntary access control (like unlocking your own phone)
- Opt-in verification services
- Security in limited, high-risk environments

**Key point:** Companies usually need your explicit, freely given consent to use your biometric data to identify you; other legal grounds are narrow. A sign saying "facial recognition in use" is not enough: you must have a real choice.

## Know Your Rights

### Your Rights in Public Spaces

**Right to know** - Public authorities using facial recognition must be transparent about it

**Right to object** - You can object to processing of your biometric data

**Right to remedies** - If your rights are violated, you can file complaints and seek damages

### Your Rights with Private Companies

**Right to refuse** - Consent must be freely given: if facial recognition isn't necessary for the service, refusing it should not cost you the service (GDPR Art. 7(4)).

**Right to deletion** - You can request your biometric data be deleted

**Right to access** - You can ask what biometric data is held about you

## What You Can Do

### If You Encounter Facial Recognition

**Ask questions:**
- What is this system being used for?
- Is it mandatory?
- What happens to my data?
- How long is it stored?

**Know your options:**
- Request alternative methods (ID card, PIN, attendant verification)
- File a complaint if consent wasn't properly obtained

### If You're Concerned About Public Surveillance

**Check local policies** - Your municipality should publish information about any surveillance systems in use

**Contact your data protection authority** - They can investigate unlawful surveillance. In some countries, such as Denmark, the data protection authority also supervises certain AI Act bans.

**Support civil society organisations** - Groups working on digital rights can amplify your concerns

## Country Variations

The EU AI Act sets common rules across Europe. National law can add stricter limits, especially on police use of biometric identification (AI Act Art. 5(5)).

**What this means for you:**
- Check your national data protection authority's guidance
- Rules may be stricter where you live
- Enforcement may look different from country to country

## What Happens to Organisations That Break the Rules?

Using a banned AI practice can lead to fines of up to **€35 million or 7% of a company's worldwide annual turnover**, whichever is higher. For SMEs and start-ups, the lower of the two amounts applies. Enforcement powers have been live since August 2026, but some EU countries were late in appointing their authorities, so enforcement is still getting started.

## Quick Reference

- **Real-time police identification in public spaces** → ❌ Banned (narrow exceptions)
- **Scraping faces from internet/CCTV** → ❌ Banned
- **Emotion detection at work/school** → ❌ Banned
- **Inferring race, religion, sexual orientation, etc. from biometrics** → ❌ Banned
- **Emotion detection in recruitment** → ❌ Banned
- **Post-event law enforcement** → ⚠️ Not banned; high-risk rules from 2 Dec 2027
- **Emotion recognition / biometric categorisation where still allowed** → 🔔 You must be told
- **Voluntary phone/device unlock** → ✅ Allowed with consent
- **Building access with consent** → ✅ Allowed with consent
