# About the EU AI Act

The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act (EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) sets rules for building, selling and using certain AI systems in the EU. It takes a risk-based approach: the more an AI system can affect people's safety or fundamental rights, the stronger the rules.

In 2026 the Act was amended by the "Digital Omnibus on AI" (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744). That change moved some deadlines, mainly for high-risk systems, and added a new ban. This page reflects the law as amended.

This page is a plain-language summary for orientation, last checked on 6 November 2026. It is not legal advice. For the exact wording, see the official texts listed under Sources.

## What the Act is trying to achieve

At its heart, the AI Act is about making sure AI works for people, not only for the organisations that build or sell it. In practical terms it aims to:

- **Ban a small number of AI practices** that are considered unacceptable
- **Set requirements for "high-risk" uses**, where AI can shape someone's job, education, money, safety or freedom
- **Make AI visible**, so people know when they are talking to a machine or looking at AI-generated content
- **Give everyone in the EU the same baseline** for supervision and enforcement

The law cannot do this alone. Whether AI actually serves people depends on the humans who choose, configure, check and question it every day.

## Risk-based classification (simplified)

### Prohibited practices ("unacceptable risk")

Since 2 February 2025, eight types of AI practice are banned in the EU. Examples include:

- Social scoring that leads to unfair treatment
- Manipulative or exploitative techniques that cause significant harm
- Emotion recognition at work and in education (including recruitment), except for medical or safety reasons
- Real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement purposes (by police or on their behalf), with narrow exceptions

From **2 December 2026**, a ninth ban applies: AI systems that generate or manipulate realistic intimate or sexually explicit images of identifiable people without their consent, including child sexual abuse material (so-called "nudifier" apps). Read more on [intimate deepfakes](/en/rights/intimate-deepfakes).

### High-risk systems

Some AI systems are classed as "high-risk" and must meet stronger requirements. The list in Annex III (the list of high-risk areas in the law) covers areas such as:

- Biometrics
- Critical infrastructure
- Education and vocational training
- Employment and worker management, including hiring
- Access to essential public and private services, including credit scoring and benefits
- Law enforcement, migration and border control
- Justice and democratic processes

**These high-risk rules do not apply yet.** After the 2026 amendment, they apply from 2 December 2027 for the areas above, and from 2 August 2028 for Annex I systems (AI built into products already covered by EU safety laws, such as medical devices or toys). Until then, GDPR and other existing laws still protect you.

### Transparency obligations ("limited risk")

Since 2 August 2026, some AI systems must be transparent with the people who use them or are affected by them:

- Chatbots must make clear that you are dealing with an AI, unless it is obvious from the context
- Deepfakes and AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest must be labelled
- Users of emotion recognition or biometric categorisation systems must inform the people exposed to them

See [your right to know when AI is involved](/en/rights/ai-transparency).

### Other systems ("minimal risk")

Many everyday AI applications, such as spam filters or AI features in games, have no special AI Act obligations beyond general EU law. One rule applies to every organisation using AI, whatever the risk: **AI literacy**. Providers and deployers must take measures to support the AI literacy of their staff.

## Compliance and enforcement

For high-risk systems (once the rules apply), typical requirements include:

- Risk management and quality management
- Good data governance and documentation
- Clear information for the people using the system
- Real human oversight, by people who understand the system and can step in
- Accuracy, robustness, security and ongoing monitoring

Maximum fines are set by the Act:

- Prohibited practices: up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher
- Most other obligations, including transparency: up to €15 million or 3%
- Giving incorrect information to authorities: up to €7.5 million or 1%
- For SMEs (including start-ups), the lower of the two amounts applies; for "small mid-caps", the lower amount applies except for banned practices

Supervision and enforcement powers are live since 2 August 2026, but many EU countries were late in naming their national authorities. These are maximum amounts; we are not aware of any AI Act fine having been imposed so far.

## Timeline

The AI Act applies in phases. Here is where things stand on 6 November 2026. For more detail, see the full [AI Act timeline](/en/timeline).

| Date | What applies | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Aug 2024 | AI Act enters into force | Done |
| 2 Feb 2025 | Banned practices (bans 1 to 8) and AI literacy | In force |
| 2 Aug 2025 | Rules for general-purpose AI models, EU governance (AI Office, AI Board), penalties regime; deadline for countries to name national authorities | In force |
| 27 Jul 2026 | Digital Omnibus on AI enters into force; new AI literacy article and new article on using sensitive data to detect bias | In force |
| 2 Aug 2026 | General application: transparency rules for chatbots, deepfakes, emotion recognition and biometric categorisation; supervision and enforcement by the AI Office and national authorities; Commission can fine general-purpose AI providers | In force |
| 2 Dec 2026 | New ban on non-consensual intimate deepfakes and child sexual abuse material; end of grace period for machine-readable marking by generative AI systems already on the market before 2 Aug 2026 | Coming in weeks |
| 2 Aug 2027 | Each EU country must have at least one AI regulatory sandbox running | Coming |
| 2 Dec 2027 | High-risk rules for Annex III areas (biometrics, infrastructure, education, employment, essential services, law enforcement, migration, justice). Previously 2 Aug 2026 | Coming |
| 2 Aug 2028 | High-risk rules for AI in regulated products (e.g. medical devices, toys, lifts, vehicles). Previously 2 Aug 2027 | Coming |
| 2 Aug 2030 | Deadline for high-risk AI systems already used by public authorities | Coming |

## Why this matters

**If you are a citizen**, the Act already gives you some protection: certain harmful practices are banned, and you should be told when you are talking to a chatbot or looking at a labelled deepfake. For decisions about jobs, credit or benefits, GDPR remains your main tool today, including the right to an explanation and human review where a decision is made solely by automated means and has legal or similarly significant effects. See [your rights](/en/rights) and the [right to explanation](/en/rights/right-to-explanation).

**If you build, buy or use AI in an organisation**, a basic understanding helps you:

- check whether a use case may be regulated now or later
- use the extra time before the high-risk rules apply to prepare well, not to wait
- train your people, because AI literacy and real human oversight are what make AI trustworthy in practice

Good AI governance is not just paperwork. It is how you make sure the technology helps the people it touches.
